\section{Introduction}
Formula Student (FS) is an international engineering competition in which university teams design, build and race a single-seat car; in the driverless (DV) class, the same car must complete the dynamic events autonomously. Every dynamic event is driven on a track that exists only as a pattern of traffic cones on an open area of tarmac. Under the 2026 driverless specification, the left border is marked with small blue cones, the right border with small yellow cones, entry and exit lanes with small orange cones, and big orange cones are placed before and after the start, finish and timekeeping lines, with at most 5~m between consecutive cones in the driving direction and shorter spacing in corners~\cite{fsds2026}. Teams reproduce these layouts many times per season on their own test sites: acceleration and skidpad layouts for vehicle tuning, and autocross and trackdrive circuits of a few hundred metres, which already need well over a hundred cones per lap.

These tracks are usually set out by hand. A plan drawn on paper or in a spreadsheet is transferred to the ground with measuring tapes and trigonometry, which needs several people, takes time away from testing and provides no check of the result: a misplaced or wrong-coloured cone is only corrected if someone notices it. For driverless cars, the consequences go beyond inconvenience. Their perception and mapping pipelines use the cones as the landmarks that define the track, and path planning and control are built on the map estimated from them~\cite{kabzan2020}. A layout that differs from the intended one therefore changes the problem the car is solving and makes results from different test days hard to compare. Surveying instruments such as total stations and real-time kinematic (RTK) global navigation satellite system (GNSS) receivers can set out points with centimetre accuracy, but they are expensive and require trained operators \TODO{cite a surveying source}. None of these options links the plan, the placement on the field and a record of what was actually built.

Augmented reality (AR) combines virtual content with the user's view of the real world, registered in three dimensions and interactive in real time~\cite{azuma1997}. Mobile AR frameworks on consumer smartphones, such as Google's ARCore, estimate the pose of the device by combining visual feature points from the camera with inertial measurements~\cite{arcore}. This makes it possible to show each planned cone, with its colour, at its position on the ground through the phone that team members already carry. Compared with the conventional alternatives (a tape measure, or a 2D map showing the phone's GNSS position), AR guidance removes the measuring step, shows the instruction where the cone must be placed, and allows the same camera to verify the placement. Evidence from construction point layout shows both the potential and the main limitation: with a head-mounted AR device registered to a paper marker, Chalhoub and Ayer reported a locational accuracy of about 5~cm, which became worse further from the marker~\cite{chalhoub2019}. Their indoor test area was much smaller than an FS test site, so this accuracy cannot be assumed to hold over a full track.

The proposed system, TrackSeed, combines the contributions of the two course units around a shared model of the track. Virtual and Augmented Reality (VAR) provides the AR guidance aligned to the real ground and a digital twin (DT) of the track. Following Kritzinger et al.~\cite{kritzinger2018}, the term DT is used here only for a virtual counterpart that exchanges data automatically with its physical counterpart in both directions: the TrackSeed twin receives every confirmed cone from the phones and sends track edits and section assignments back to them. Emerging and Intelligent Technologies (EIT) provides the cloud platform that stores track plans and placement sessions, real-time synchronisation between the phones and a web dashboard, remote configuration of the devices, and on-device intelligence that checks each placed cone with the camera.

This paper reviews the state of the art that TrackSeed builds on, with three objectives: (i) to establish what accuracy mobile AR achieves for on-site layout and guidance, and which alignment approaches limit drift over large outdoor areas; (ii) to compare architectures for a bidirectional DT synchronised in real time between mobile and web clients; and (iii) to derive and justify the technology choices and validation plan of the proposal. Section~II reviews the literature by theme, Section~III analyses the candidate technologies, Section~IV presents the proposed solution and its validation plan, Section~V discusses feasibility and limitations, and Section~VI concludes.
