\begin{abstract}
Formula Student (FS) tracks are marked with hundreds of colour-coded cones that teams set out by hand with tapes and trigonometry, a slow process with no check of the result, although driverless cars depend on the layout matching the plan. This paper reviews the state of the art for TrackSeed, a system in which one person sets out a track with augmented reality (AR) guidance on a smartphone while a cloud-hosted digital twin (DT) follows the placement live. The review covers AR for on-site layout, mobile AR tracking and alignment to the ground, DT definitions, real-time synchronisation and on-device inference, using studies selected for quantitative evidence and official documentation for tool capabilities. The literature shows that AR placement reaches centimetre-to-decimetre accuracy over small areas, with phones the least accurate devices, and that tracking error grows with the distance from the reference; no study has evaluated phone-based setout over a full track. Based on these findings, TrackSeed adopts two-point marker alignment with re-alignment along the track, WebSocket synchronisation with an offline queue, a two-way twin and on-device cone verification. Placement accuracy, setup time and synchronisation latency are proposed to validate it.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Formula Student (FS) is an international engineering competition in which university teams design, build and race a single-seat car; in the driverless (DV) class, the same car must complete the dynamic events autonomously. Every dynamic event is driven on a track that exists only as a pattern of color coded cones on an open area of tarmac. Under the 2026 driverless specification, the left border is marked with small blue cones, the right border with small yellow cones, entry and exit lanes with small orange cones, and big orange cones are placed before and after the start, finish and timekeeping lines, with at most 5~m between consecutive cones in the driving direction and shorter spacing in corners~\cite{fsds2026}. In the authors' FS team, these layouts are reproduced many times per season on the team's own test site: acceleration and skidpad layouts for vehicle tuning, and autocross and trackdrive circuits of a few hundred metres, which already need well over a hundred cones per lap.

In the authors' team, these tracks are set out by hand: a plan drawn on paper or in a spreadsheet is transferred to the ground with measuring tapes and trigonometry, which needs several people, takes time away from testing and provides no check of the result: a misplaced or wrong-coloured cone is only corrected if someone notices it. For driverless cars, the consequences go beyond inconvenience. Their perception and mapping pipelines use the cones as the landmarks that define the track, and path planning and control are built on the map estimated from them~\cite{kabzan2020}. A layout that differs from the intended one changes the problem the car is solving and makes results from different test days hard to compare. Surveying instruments such as total stations and real-time kinematic (RTK) global navigation satellite system (GNSS) receivers reach centimetre accuracy~\cite{jiang2023}, but they are dedicated equipment that, in the authors' experience, student teams rarely own or are trained to use. None of these options links the plan, the placement on the field and a record of what was actually built.

Augmented reality (AR) combines virtual content with the user's view of the real world, registered in three dimensions and interactive in real time~\cite{azuma1997}. Mobile AR frameworks on consumer smartphones, such as Google's ARCore, estimate the pose of the device by combining visual feature points from the camera with inertial measurements~\cite{arcore}. This makes it possible to show each planned cone, with its colour, at its position on the ground through a smartphone, so that a single team member can set out the track alone. Compared with the conventional alternatives (a tape measure, or a 2D map showing the phone's GNSS position), AR guidance removes the measuring step, shows the instruction where the cone must be placed, and allows the same camera to verify the placement. Evidence from construction point layout shows both the potential and the main limitation: with a head-mounted AR device registered to a paper marker, Chalhoub and Ayer reported a locational accuracy of about 5~cm, which became worse further from the marker~\cite{chalhoub2019}. Their indoor test area was much smaller than an FS test site, so this accuracy cannot be assumed to hold over a full track.

The proposed system, TrackSeed, combines the contributions of the two course units around a shared model of the track. Virtual and Augmented Reality (VAR) provides the AR guidance aligned to the real ground and a digital twin (DT) of the track. Following Kritzinger et al.~\cite{kritzinger2018}, the term DT is used here only for a virtual counterpart that exchanges data automatically with its physical counterpart in both directions: the TrackSeed twin receives every cone confirmed by the phone and sends track edits made on the dashboard back to it. Emerging and Intelligent Technologies (EIT) provides the cloud platform that stores track plans and placement sessions, real-time synchronisation between the phone and a web dashboard, remote configuration of the app, and on-device intelligence that checks each placed cone with the camera. In this first version, one person places the cones with one phone; collaborative placement with several phones is left for future work.

This paper reviews the state of the art that TrackSeed builds on, with three objectives: (i) to establish what accuracy mobile AR achieves for on-site layout and guidance, and which alignment approaches limit drift over large outdoor areas; (ii) to compare architectures for a bidirectional DT synchronised in real time between mobile and web clients; and (iii) to derive and justify the technology choices and validation plan of the proposal. Section~II reviews the literature by theme, Section~III analyses the candidate technologies, Section~IV presents the proposed solution and its validation plan, Section~V discusses feasibility and limitations, and Section~VI concludes.

\subsection{Scope and literature search}
The literature was searched in October 2026 in Google Scholar and in publishers' digital libraries, assisted by an AI tool (see the AI use declaration) and by following the references and citations of the most relevant studies. Search strings were built from the main concepts of the problem (AR, on-site layout, mobile tracking accuracy and digital twins) and their synonyms, for example (``augmented reality'' OR ``mixed reality'') AND (``layout'' OR ``setout'' OR ``stakeout'') AND (``accuracy'' OR ``error''), and (``ARCore'' OR ``mobile AR'') AND (``drift'' OR ``accuracy'') AND ``outdoor''. A source was kept only if it was directly relevant to the problem, reported evidence that could be compared, came from a credible venue, was recent enough to reflect current devices, and helped compare alternatives or justify a design decision; peer-reviewed papers published from 2015 onwards were preferred. Foundational works were kept for definitions, and official documentation and the FS rules were used only for tool capabilities and domain requirements, not as evidence of performance. Since almost no studies address track setout in motorsport, the review relies mainly on adjacent domains, construction layout in particular, whose tasks, distances and accuracy requirements differ from those of an FS test site. This limits how directly their results transfer, as discussed in Section~V. The final selection comprises 22 sources: 16 peer-reviewed papers, chapters and reviews, one preprint, and five specifications or official documentation pages.

\subsection{AR for on-site layout and spatial guidance}
Construction layout, where workers mark the positions of building elements from a design model, is the task closest to setting out a track, and it is the domain where AR placement has been measured most directly. The studies found differ mainly in the device used and in the size and type of the test area.

\textit{Head-mounted AR in small indoor areas.} Chalhoub and Ayer asked industry practitioners to lay out electrical points with a HoloLens registered to a paper marker, and measured each point with a laser distance meter~\cite{chalhoub2019}. Content was displayed about 5~cm from its design position, and the error was smaller near the marker. Showing more content, or content of different types, also reduced accuracy in elevation. The test was carried out indoors, over an area much smaller than a track.

\textit{Head-mounted, handheld and GNSS-assisted AR outdoors.} Kim and Olsen compared four devices in an outdoor, partly shaded simulation of underground utility placement, with 32 construction students placing survey prisms where they perceived each virtual pipe and a robotic total station recording the positions~\cite{kim2025isarc}. The mean error was 3.28~in (about 8.3~cm) with a HoloLens~2, 3.52~in (8.9~cm) with Trimble SiteVision, which adds a GNSS receiver, 3.59~in (9.1~cm) with an iPad Pro and 6.26~in (15.9~cm) with an iPhone~13 Pro Max, with eight observations per device and pipe. An earlier report on the same scenario also found the HoloLens to be the most accurate device and concluded that the devices did not meet the strict tolerances of construction work~\cite{kim2024ahfe}.

The two groups of studies agree that AR placement reaches centimetre-to-decimetre accuracy over small areas, that head-mounted devices are more accurate than handheld ones, and that accuracy depends on the distance from the reference used to register the content. They differ by a factor of about two in the error they report, which can be explained by the indoor versus outdoor setting and by the participants' experience, so the values cannot be compared directly. For TrackSeed, the most relevant figure is the phone result: an error of about 16~cm outdoors is close to the provisional tolerance of 10--15~cm assumed for an FS track, and it was obtained over a much smaller area than a track. The literature therefore supports AR guidance as feasible, but not as accurate enough by default; the accuracy of phone-based setout over a full track is the main hypothesis TrackSeed has to test.

\subsection{Mobile AR tracking accuracy and alignment to the ground}
Placement accuracy over a full track depends on how well the phone keeps its position as the user walks, and on how the virtual plan is aligned to the real ground. Three approaches appear in the literature: relying on the phone's own visual-inertial tracking after an initial alignment, localising the phone against a visual map, and using satellite positioning.

\textit{Visual-inertial tracking alone.} ARCore estimates the phone's pose from camera features combined with inertial measurements~\cite{arcore}. In a 1{,}600~m\textsuperscript{2} industrial hall with walking distances of 60~m, Feigl et al.\ found that ARCore, ARKit and HoloLens accumulated an average error of about 17~m per 120~m walked, with a scale error of up to 14.4~cm/m that grew roughly in proportion to the path length~\cite{feigl2020}. At room scale, Scargill et al.\ measured the drift of virtual objects on three phones in more than 200 environments and found errors of a few centimetres that depended on what the user did between views: when the user walked away and returned, the mean drift was 4.8~cm with ARCore and 25.1~cm with ARKit~\cite{scargill2021}. Drift also increased in dim scenes and in scenes with little texture or low contrast.

\textit{Visual positioning.} The ARCore Geospatial API localises the phone against imagery from Google Street View and is described by Google as more accurate than GPS alone, but the documentation does not give accuracy figures and the service only works where Street View coverage exists~\cite{arcoregeo}. Skabek et al.\ compared it with other AR localisation platforms for indoor and outdoor campus navigation~\cite{skabek2026}, a use case whose accuracy needs are far looser than cone placement. No independent evaluation at the centimetre level was found.

\textit{Satellite positioning.} Standard phone GNSS is not accurate enough for cone placement, but RTK corrections change this: Jiang et al.\ reached root-mean-square errors of 1.1--2.1~cm in a static test and about 4~cm in a kinematic test with unmodified smartphones in open-sky conditions~\cite{jiang2023}. In the outdoor study of Kim and Olsen, the GNSS-assisted SiteVision was more accurate than both handheld devices but not better than the HoloLens~\cite{kim2025isarc}.

The two tracking studies appear to disagree, with errors of metres in one and centimetres in the other, but the difference is explained by scale: the error of visual-inertial tracking grows with the distance walked~\cite{feigl2020}, and a room-scale study cannot reveal it. Both agree that the environment matters, and Scargill et al.'s finding that low texture increases drift is a risk for the uniform asphalt of a test site, although no study measured it on tarmac. The trade-off is clear: visual tracking is free and works anywhere but drifts over distance; visual positioning depends on map coverage and lacks published accuracy; RTK GNSS gives centimetre accuracy without drift but needs a receiver, a correction source and an open sky, which an FS test site usually provides. For TrackSeed, these findings mean that a single alignment at the start line cannot keep a track of several hundred metres within tolerance, and that the plan must be re-aligned at reference points along the track or anchored to absolute positions.

\subsection{Digital twins}
Grieves and Vickers describe the digital twin as three parts: a physical product, its virtual counterpart and the data connection between them~\cite{grieves2017}. Kritzinger et al.\ classified the manufacturing literature by how that connection works: a \textit{digital model} exchanges no data automatically, a \textit{digital shadow} is updated automatically from the physical object, and only a \textit{digital twin} exchanges data automatically in both directions~\cite{kritzinger2018}. They found that most publications describe models or shadows, and that literature on the full twin is scarce. Tao et al.\ reviewed the state of the art of DT in industry based on 50 papers~\cite{tao2019}.

The reviews agree that the term DT is often used for systems that are only shadows, and that full twins are rare outside manufacturing; none of the sources reviewed applies a DT to track setout. For TrackSeed, Kritzinger et al.'s classification sets a concrete requirement: a dashboard that only displays the cones confirmed by the phone would be a digital shadow. To be a twin, changes made in the virtual track, such as a moved or added cone, must reach the phone automatically and change the guidance on the field.

\subsection{Real-time platforms and synchronisation}
Live updates between the phone, the cloud and the dashboard can follow a client--server model, typically WebSocket connections, or a publish--subscribe model such as MQTT~\cite{ajayi2023}.\ compared both for streaming IoT telemetry and found that MQTT achieved higher throughput and used fewer system resources, while WebSocket had slightly lower round-trip times~\cite{ajayi2023}. The evidence found for this theme is limited to a single controlled comparison with telemetry data, so it shows a trade-off rather than a general ranking. For TrackSeed, the volume of data is small (one message per confirmed cone) and the receiving end is a web dashboard, which favours WebSocket's direct browser support and lower latency over MQTT's efficiency at scale; this is assessed further in Section~III.

\subsection{Edge AI for on-site verification}
TrackSeed checks each placed cone with a neural network, which can run in the cloud, on the phone or in a hybrid of both.The trade-off being: sending camera frames to the cloud gives access to more computing power but adds latencyand privacy risks while using uplink bandwidth and exposes the images to privacy risks, while on-device inference avoids these costs but is limited by the device's computing power and memory~\cite{chen2019}.Frameworks such as TensorFlow Lite (now LiteRT) provide on-device inference and mobile hardware has narrowed the gap: in a benchmark of smartphone AI accelerators, newest mobile neural processing units were approaching the performance of CUDA-compatible Nvidia graphics cards~\cite{ignatov2019}.

For the task itself, FS teams have built large shared datasets for cone detection. FSOCO provides human-annotated bounding boxes and segmentation masks of FS cones, collected through a policy where teams contribute images before they can download the dataset~\cite{vodisch2022}. Dhall et al.\ detected cones and estimated their 3D position in real time on a low-power embedded computer mounted on a driverless car~\cite{dhall2019}. Both, however, were built for cameras mounted on a moving car, which see cones from a different height and distance than a phone held above the cone being placed. The sources agree that on-device inference is practical with compressed models, and test fields often have weak mobile coverage, in the authors' experience; together, these favour running the check on the phone and using the cloud only to retrain models and push new versions.

\subsection{Comparative synthesis}
Table~\ref{tab:literature} summarises the studies reviewed. Three findings stand out. First, every study that measured position over distance found that error grows with the distance from the reference, whether that reference is a marker~\cite{chalhoub2019} or the starting point of tracking~\cite{feigl2020}; the studies disagree on magnitude only because they test different scales. Second, almost all accuracy evidence comes from areas of a few metres to tens of metres, while an FS track covers hundreds; the only large-scale study measured errors far above any useful tolerance without re-alignment~\cite{feigl2020}. Third, phones are consistently less accurate than head-mounted devices~\cite{kim2025isarc}, yet phones are the only devices FS teams have available. What cannot be concluded from the literature is whether phone-based AR, combined with re-alignment or RTK positioning, keeps a full track within tolerance: no study has tested this. No work was found that combines placement guidance, automatic verification of each placed object and a live two-way digital twin, which is the gap TrackSeed addresses.

\subsection{Aligning the track plan to the ground}
Table~\ref{tab:alignment} compares the alignment options against R1 and R2. A single marker at the start line is the simplest option, but it fixes the heading of the whole plan from a marker less than a metre wide: a heading error of only $1^\circ$ moves a cone 200~m away by about 3.5~m, and visual-inertial drift adds to this as the user walks~\cite{feigl2020}. Aligning on two markers placed far apart fixes position and heading together, and re-aligning at further markers along the track limits the drift to the distance between markers, consistent with the finding that error is smallest near the reference~\cite{chalhoub2019}. The Geospatial API is rejected as the main method because no evidence shows it reaches the required accuracy~\cite{arcoregeo}. RTK GNSS reaches centimetre accuracy without drift~\cite{jiang2023}, but needs a correction service and suitable hardware, so it is kept as an optional extension. TrackSeed therefore adopts two-point marker alignment with re-alignment markers. Two decisions remain open: the spacing between markers, to be set from field tests, and how the markers are positioned, which only needs to be done once per site with the team's usual method or an RTK receiver.

\subsection{Mobile AR platform}
Native Android with ARCore, Unity with AR Foundation and native iOS with ARKit were considered. Native Android in Kotlin gives direct access to the ARCore session and its camera frames, which the on-device cone check needs, and to the sensors and local storage needed for offline work (R3). Unity would add cross-platform reach, which this version does not need (R2), at the cost of an extra runtime layer between the camera and the model. Tracking quality does not decide the choice: Scargill et al.\ found neither platform uniformly better, and ARCore drifted less when the user walked away and returned~\cite{scargill2021}, the movement most similar to walking between cones. The compromise is that the app runs only on ARCore-supported Android phones; other users reach the twin through the web dashboard.

\subsection{Platform and real-time communication}
Table~\ref{tab:comms} compares the options for synchronising the phone, the cloud and the dashboard. TrackSeed sends one small message per confirmed cone, and the dashboard runs in a browser. In this setting, WebSocket's lower latency~\cite{ajayi2023} and native browser support outweigh MQTT's higher throughput and lower resource use, which matter for many constrained devices rather than one phone. A managed real-time database would provide offline synchronisation out of the box~\cite{firebaseoffline}, but it ties the platform to one provider and gives less control over the twin's data model. TrackSeed therefore uses WebSocket for live updates and REST for uploading and downloading tracks and reports. To meet R3, the phone stores the track and every confirmed cone locally and replays the queued messages when the connection returns, the same pattern a managed database applies internally. The services run in Docker containers, and tracks use an open JSON schema so that plans from other FS tools can be imported.

\subsection{Digital twin and on-device intelligence}
The twin stores, for every cone, its planned position and colour and a state: planned, placed, verified, wrong colour or missing, with the measured offset. Because cones lie on a plane, a 2D top-down map is enough for the dashboard; a 3D view is optional. Edits made on the dashboard go through the sync service to the phone and change the guidance, which makes the system a twin rather than a shadow in the sense of Kritzinger et al.~\cite{kritzinger2018}. Following Section~II-E, the cone check runs on the phone with a compressed model in LiteRT~\cite{chen2019}, while the cloud stores training data and pushes new model versions, which also provides remote configuration. A second on-device model, developed in the Machine and Deep Learning course, recognises the team car at the end of a session for the report.

\section{Project Proposal}
\subsection{Solution and conceptual architecture}
Before a test day, the team lead uploads a track plan, a JSON file with the position and colour of every cone, through the web dashboard. On the field, one team member opens the track on an ARCore-supported Android phone, aligns it by scanning two printed markers at the start line, and walks the track while the app shows the position and colour of each cone in AR. When a cone is put down, the on-device model checks that it is present, has the right colour and lies within tolerance, and the phone sends the result to the cloud. The team lead follows the twin live on the dashboard and can move, add or remove cones, which immediately updates the guidance on the phone. At the end, the user photographs the car that will run, and the app produces a session report with the track, the car, the setup time and the placement accuracy.

Fig.~\ref{fig:architecture} shows the four components and the course unit behind each part. The Android app (DMD) runs the AR alignment and guidance (VAR), the cone check and car recognition (EIT, MDL) and the offline store. The cloud platform (EIT) holds the REST API, the WebSocket sync service, the database with the twin state and the model store. The web dashboard (EIT, VAR) shows and edits the twin. Tracking and inference run on the phone; storage, synchronisation, validation of uploaded tracks and model training run in the cloud. The system depends on an ARCore-supported Android phone, printed markers, a host for the containers, and the FSOCO dataset~\cite{vodisch2022} plus the team's own images for training. Several phones placing cones at once, iOS, and the Geospatial API as the main alignment method are out of scope. The preliminary plan is: October, track format and AR alignment on a skidpad; November, on-device verification, cloud platform and synchronisation; December, dashboard twin and full autocross tests; January, validation and report. The marker spacing and the model architecture remain provisional.


\subsection{Preliminary validation}

\section{Discussion}
The proposal is feasible within one semester because it combines existing, documented technologies (ARCore, LiteRT, WebSocket) rather than developing new tracking or networking methods, and because the single-placer scope avoids the problem of aligning several phones. The main technical risk is accuracy over distance: the evidence shows error growing with the distance from the reference~\cite{chalhoub2019}, \cite{feigl2020}, and no study measured phone AR on asphalt, a low-texture surface that is likely to increase drift~\cite{scargill2021}. Outdoor lighting, glare on the screen and wet weather during winter tests may also affect both tracking and the cone check, whose training data come from car-mounted cameras~\cite{vodisch2022}. The user's safety is a further concern, as attention is on the screen while walking; the app is meant to be used only on a closed track.

The evidence itself has limits. Most accuracy studies come from construction, indoors or over small areas, with few participants~\cite{chalhoub2019}, \cite{kim2025isarc}; one tracking study is a preprint~\cite{scargill2021}; and the protocol comparison is a single controlled test~\cite{ajayi2023}. The review changed the proposal in three ways: a single start marker was replaced by two-point alignment with re-alignment markers, the Geospatial API was moved from a candidate to a rejected option, and the dashboard was required to send edits back to the phone so that the system is a true twin. Unity, iOS and dedicated surveying instruments were rejected for this version; collaborative placement with shared anchors and an integrated RTK receiver are left for future work. The expected contribution is a justified integration and evaluation of existing technologies for a task that has not been studied, not a new tracking method.

\section{Conclusions}
This paper reviewed the evidence behind TrackSeed, an AR system for setting out FS tracks with a live digital twin. The literature shows that AR placement reaches centimetre-to-decimetre accuracy over small areas, that phones are the least accurate devices, and that tracking error grows with the distance from the reference; no study has evaluated phone-based setout over a full track. These findings led to two-point marker alignment with re-alignment, WebSocket synchronisation with an offline queue, a two-way twin and on-device cone verification. The next steps are to implement AR alignment on a skidpad, measure the drift on the team's test site to set the marker spacing, and then validate placement accuracy, setup time and synchronisation latency on a full track.
